A short primer

Six things to understand before you go anywhere near China.

Written for established Western brands weighing the market — and for hotels and destinations wondering why Chinese travellers aren't booking. It isn't everything. But every brand we have seen struggle in China misjudged at least one of these.

Your name comes first — and so does your trademark

Your brand will have a Chinese name whether you choose one or not: consumers, distributors, travel agents and the press will coin one for you, and it will spread on RED before you've noticed. There are three ways to build a good one. Phonetic — keep the sound, let the foreignness be the asset (Häagen-Dazs 哈根达斯). Semantic — drop the sound, keep the meaning (Lotte 乐天, “joyful and heavenly”). Hybrid — keep the sound skeleton and load the characters with meaning (Magnum 梦龙, “dream dragon”; Coca-Cola 可口可乐). The hybrid is the highest-ceiling play, and the hardest to get right.

Whatever the route, the name must pass the three-second test — a stranger should know what you are and roughly what you sell at a glance — and it must be registered before anyone else registers it. China is first-to-file: whoever files first owns the mark, regardless of who used it first elsewhere. Register the Latin and Chinese names, in every relevant class, with defensive variants held in reserve. Examination takes months, so this starts before anything is public.

Choose your entry route deliberately

There are several ways in, each with a different cost, control and speed. Cross-border e-commerce lets you sell from overseas via Tmall Global or JD Worldwide without a Chinese entity or full product registration — the fastest way to get real demand data. General trade means importing, registering and selling domestically: more compliance, full market access. A distributor or licensing partner lowers your risk and your control in equal measure. For hotels, the route is different again: it runs through WeChat, RED, the OTAs and the luxury travel trade. Many brands start with one route and graduate to another — the mistake is drifting into one by default.

The channels are not the ones you know — and AI is now one of them

There is no Amazon, Instagram or Google. Commerce runs through Tmall and JD and, increasingly, Douyin livestreams, where a host can sell more in an evening than a store does in a quarter. Discovery happens on Xiaohongshu (RED) and WeChat. Hotels are booked through Ctrip and Mini Programs, not your website, which most Chinese consumers can't even load.

And there is a new layer on top: Chinese consumers now ask AI assistants — Doubao, DeepSeek, Kimi, Yuanbao, Tongyi Qianwen — what to buy and where to stay. More than 90% of that traffic runs on domestic models. They already have an opinion about your brand. You almost certainly don't know what it is.

Localise the offer, not just the label

Gifting seasons (Chinese New Year, Mid-Autumn, 11.11), pack sizes, price ladders, flavour and colour preferences, and the story a brand tells about its heritage all differ. So does the competition: domestic brands are now faster, more digital and often better at telling a Chinese story than the imports. Premium brands succeed when they keep their authenticity and adapt how it is expressed — not by becoming Chinese, and not by ignoring the market.

Compliance is real, slow, and not optional

Product registration, labelling, import licences, food and cosmetics testing, regulated payments if you want to sell gift cards or vouchers, data rules, and the mechanics of setting up and running an entity all take longer than first-timers expect. None of it can be skipped and all of it has to be sequenced into the plan from the start.

Nobody succeeds with a supplier. You need a partner who stays.

China rewards patience and punishes half-commitment. The brands that build lasting businesses treat the first two years as investment, measure against a multi-year plan, and have someone accountable on the ground — not a rotating cast of agencies, each responsible for its own slice and none for the whole. The most common failure we see isn't a bad strategy. It's a good strategy with nobody left to run it.
